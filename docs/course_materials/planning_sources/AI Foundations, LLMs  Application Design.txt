=== PAGE 16 ===
Predictive vs. Generative AI
Predictive AI: input → a label, a number, or a probability.
Is this transaction fraudulent? What will demand be next
week?
Generative AI: input → new content.
Draft a reply to this customer. Summarize this contract.
Both must be evaluated: a forecast always carries
uncertainty; a generated answer can be fluent, plausible,
and wrong - and is harder to check.
10

=== PAGE 17 ===
Most AI already running in companies is predictive: credit scoring, demand forecasting, fraud detection. It is evaluated against known
outcomes: error rates, and how well its probabilities match reality.
Generative models do thousands of tasks with one model, but their outputs are open-ended, with no single correct answer: evaluating
them is harder (Sessions 4 and 5).
This distinction is the key to the claims-handling activity.

=== PAGE 26 ===
Next-Token Prediction
Tokens (words or word pieces): what you pay for; they limit the context window.
Our | quarterly | results | were                    → 4 tokens
Nos | résultats | trimes | tr | iels | étaient      → 6 tokens
Images, audio, and video become tokens too: multimodal models.
An LLM predicts the next token given the text so far, appends it, and repeats:
Our quarterly results were               → strong
Our quarterly results were strong        → ,
Our quarterly results were strong,       → driven
The next token is the most likely one or is sampled (depending on settings), so answers
can vary.
15

=== PAGE 27 ===
Each new token depends on everything before it: a 500-word answer takes 500+ steps, so longer answers are slower and cost more.
The split shown is that of OpenAI’s GPT-4o tokenizer (o200k_base); each model has its own. Common English words are one token each;
the French translation of the same sentence needs six, because “trimestriels” is split into pieces.
About ¾ of an English word per token (
); languages less represented in training data need more tokens. Frontier context windows
reach about a million tokens.
Always taking the most likely token is deterministic; sampling according to the probabilities adds variety. Chat assistants usually sample,
which is why their answers vary.
Frontier models read images and documents, and many listen and speak: an application can work with scans and photos, not only typed
text.

=== PAGE 28 ===
Attention, Intuitively
Before 2017: language models read text one word at a time,
passing a running summary along.
Distant words fade from the summary, and training is
slow: each word must wait for the previous one.
Attention: each word looks directly at every other word
and decides which ones matter.
The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too big.
                                                 ↑ it = the trophy
The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too small.
                                                 ↑ it = the suitcase
16

=== PAGE 29 ===
Neural language models date back to 2003 (
). Until 2017, the best ones were recurrent networks (RNNs, LSTMs): they read
a sentence word by word and compress everything seen so far into one summary. Google Translate ran on such a network from 2016
(GNMT, in the table on the next slide).
Two problems: by the end of a long text, the beginning is a faint trace in the summary; and the computation is a chain - word 10 cannot be
processed before word 9 - so it uses GPUs poorly.
Attention gives every word a direct link to every other word. Resolving “it” takes world knowledge (big things do not fit into small things):
attention lets “it” draw on “trophy” or “suitcase” depending on the last word.

=== PAGE 33 ===
Pretraining is by far the most expensive step. A base model continues “What is the capital of France?” with “What is the capital of
Germany?”: lists of questions are common on the web.
Instruction tuning: the same next-token training, but on examples of a request followed by a good answer, written or curated by people.
The model learns to answer rather than continue, and to follow the format of a conversation.
Much smaller and cheaper than pretraining: it mostly shapes behaviour, while the knowledge comes from pretraining.
With preference tuning, it is the recipe behind ChatGPT (
).
Preference tuning gives tone, refusals, and safety behaviour - and sycophancy, since people like being agreed with.
Reasoning training, on problems with checkable answers (maths, code), teaches some models to work through a problem before
answering; not every model has it, and vendors do it differently.
Your instructions are text in the model’s context: cheap to change, and possible to override (Session 4).

=== PAGE 41 ===
What LLMs Are Good At
Understand information: summarize, classify, extract, translate.
Transform information: rewrite, adapt tone, convert formats.
Generate content: drafts, ideas, explanations, reports.
Support reasoning: compare options, analyze, brainstorm.
Interact naturally: conversational interfaces.
LLMs are powerful interfaces for unstructured information. But they do not automatically
provide reliable knowledge, decisions, or actions.
23

=== PAGE 42 ===
Examples: extracting the key terms of a supplier contract; drafting a reply to a customer complaint.
Most reliable when the answer is in the input rather than in the model’s memory: summarizing a document you provide is far safer than
asking what the model “knows” about your company.
This is where LLMs are currently strongest: tasks involving language and unstructured information. However, most business applications
require more than language generation. They need access to company knowledge, the ability to perform actions, and mechanisms to
evaluate reliability. These are the topics of the next sessions.

=== PAGE 45 ===
Ground, Abstain, Verify
Hallucinations: a model produces plausible text, not
checked facts. Rare or outdated facts, missing context, and
training that rewards guessing all contribute.
Ground: give the model the source (documents, data, search
results).
Abstain: allow “I don’t know”, and say what to do instead.
Verify: ask for sources and check them; a human reviews
what matters.
25

=== PAGE 46 ===
There is no single cause.
One: training and benchmarks score “I don’t know” like a wrong answer, so models learn to guess.
On one benchmark, a model that abstains half the time is wrong 26% of the time; one that almost never abstains is wrong 75% of
the time, at similar accuracy (
; 
).
Others: facts missing, rare, or wrong in the training data; a training cutoff; ambiguous questions.
Grounding is Session 2 (and search, Session 3); abstention is an instruction-design choice (this session); verification is Sessions 4 and 5.

=== PAGE 62 ===
In an application, you write the system instructions once; users write the messages, and you do not control them.
Models are trained to rank system instructions above user messages - an “instruction hierarchy” (
) - but they do not
always comply: Session 4.
Instructions in a first message have no special priority, are easier to override later, and can get lost in long conversations. Some
assistants offer persistent settings (custom instructions, projects, Gems); how they are applied varies by vendor.

=== PAGE 63 ===
Five Principles of Instruction Design
1. Define the task, the audience, and the desired outcome.
2. Provide context, and keep instructions separate from source
material.
3. Specify the output, the constraints, and what to do when
unsure.
4. Show examples when consistency matters.
5. Test, diagnose, and refine against a fixed set of questions.
34

=== PAGE 64 ===

Where the Value Comes From
An AI application creates value through:
1. Specialization: a precise user, task, and standard of
quality.
2. Information advantage: knowledge the model does not
have.
3. Workflow integration: it acts where the work happens.
Think in tasks, not jobs: the model alone speeds up ~15% of
tasks; with software built around it, about half.
