Ground, Abstain, Verify
Hallucinations: a model produces plausible text, not
checked facts. Rare or outdated facts, missing context, and
training that rewards guessing all contribute.
Ground: give the model the source (documents, data, search
results).
Abstain: allow “I don’t know”, and say what to do instead.
Verify: ask for sources and check them; a human reviews
what matters.
4

=== PAGE 6 ===
There is no single cause.
One: training and benchmarks score “I don’t know” like a wrong answer, so models learn to guess.
On one benchmark, a model that abstains half the time is wrong 26% of the time; one that almost never abstains is wrong 75%
of the time, at similar accuracy (
; 
).
Others: facts missing, rare, or wrong in the training data; a training cutoff; ambiguous questions.
Where each comes in the course:
grounding: this session (and search, Session 3);

=== PAGE 12 ===
The three ways:
Fine-tuning: change the model’s weights, by training it further.
Context injection: put the documents themselves in the prompt (a few documents, or one long one).
Retrieval (RAG): select the relevant passages first, then put them in the prompt.
Fine-tuning is poorly suited to adding facts:
it is expensive and slow to update;
the model still cannot cite where an answer came from;
it is better at teaching behaviour (tone, format, a specialized task).
Context injection and retrieval both put evidence in front of the model at use time; they differ in what is sent, and who selects it.
Providing source material at inference time makes citations to current documents directly verifiable; fine-tuning alone does not.
Main cost:
fine-tuning: training effort;
context injection: tokens per request;
retrieval: building and tuning the pipeline.
Retrieval-augmented generation combines a search step with a language model: retrieve passages, then generate an answer conditioned
on them.
The term comes from 
, who combined a model’s “parametric” memory with a searchable index of
Wikipedia.
“Upload files” or “knowledge” features may use context injection, retrieval, or a mix, often without saying so.
Claude Projects, for example, use the documents in context when possible and switch automatically to retrieval when they approach
the context limit (
).
Most vendors do not document the mechanism. Do not assume an upload means RAG.

=== PAGE 15 ===
Full context when:
the authoritative material fits comfortably;
all relevant users may see all of it;
it changes rarely enough to manage;
cost and latency are acceptable.
Retrieval when:
the corpus is too large or changes often;
users have different access rights;
most of it is irrelevant to each question;
sending everything costs too much or is too slow;
evidence must be selected iteratively, across many sources.
More context is not free: distractors and contradictions make reasoning harder as the context grows.
The two are complementary, and often combined (Self-Route, below).
Use the simplest architecture that meets the application’s requirements.

=== PAGE 21 ===
Most RAG systems split documents into chunks, to retrieve the relevant parts rather than whole documents.
Design choices:
Size: too large, and retrieval is coarse and the model receives a lot of irrelevant text; too small, and the passage loses the context
needed to understand it.
Boundaries: split along the document’s structure (sections, articles, clauses), not every 500 words, nor in the middle of a sentence or
a table.
Overlap: repeating a few lines between neighbouring chunks avoids cutting a rule in two.
Metadata (document title, version, date, country, department, access rights): used both to find chunks and to filter them.
The fix shown on the right is the idea behind contextual retrieval (Block 2).

=== PAGE 22 ===
Embeddings, Intuitively
Chunks and question become vectors: similar meanings are
close.
Retrieval ranks chunks by closeness.
12

=== PAGE 23 ===
An embedding model turns a piece of text into a list of numbers (a vector), placed so that similar meanings are close.
Texts with similar meanings get nearby vectors, even with no words in common: “holidays I didn’t take” lands near “carry-over of
unused days”.
Real embeddings have hundreds to thousands of dimensions; the 2-D picture is only an intuition, with positions chosen by hand.
The question is embedded at query time with the same model, and the closest chunks are returned.
This is called dense or semantic retrieval.

=== PAGE 25 ===
Semantic retrieval (embeddings) handles paraphrases.
Multilingual embedding models can also bridge languages.
Lexical retrieval (keywords) handles names, codes, acronyms, product numbers, legal references.
It scores documents by the words they share with the question, weighting rare words more; BM25 is the classic scoring function
(
).
Embeddings are weak on identifiers: “HR-POL-117” and “HR-POL-171” mean nothing to them, but are different documents to the
business.
In business corpora, exact identifiers are everywhere: contract numbers, article references, product codes, employee categories.
Hybrid retrieval runs both and merges the results: a common default.
On a benchmark of 18 datasets, BM25 “is a robust baseline” that many neural retrievers fail to beat outside their training domain
(
).
Hybrid retrieval “outperforms pure-lexical and pure-semantic retrieval on most datasets” (
).

=== PAGE 27 ===
Two stages:
first stage: cheap and fast, it aims not to miss anything (recall);
reranking: slower, but more precise (precision);
the precise model is too slow to read 40,000 chunks per question, but affordable on 50.
Similar to the question is not always useful for answering it.
Many rerankers read the question and a candidate passage together and score how well the passage answers it, instead of
comparing two separately computed vectors.
Designs vary; what they share is spending more computation on fewer candidates.
The numbers are illustrative: how many candidates and how many final passages are design parameters, set by testing.
Research now trains retrievers and rerankers for usefulness to the answer rather than topical similarity.
“A retrieved document must not only be relevant but also effectively support the generator in producing correct answers” (
; see also 
).

=== PAGE 33 ===
Retrieval is an information-retrieval problem; generation is a language-model problem.
RAG joins the two: its quality is bounded by both.
For every bad answer, ask two questions:
Did the system retrieve sufficient evidence?
Given that evidence, did the model use it correctly?
To tell them apart, look at what the model received (the retrieved passages), not only at the answer.
The fix depends on the cell:
a retrieval failure is fixed in the pipeline (chunking, hybrid search, reranking, metadata);
a generation failure (the evidence was there, but the model ignored it, misread it, or mixed up passages) is fixed in the instructions,
the model, or the format of the context.
“Correct from memory” is a warning sign, not a success: the answer is unsupported and cannot be cited.
Failure points, in order: bad chunking → evidence not retrieved → relevant but insufficient → ignored or misread → contradictory or
outdated sources → absent, and the model guesses.
Each has its own fix: chunking, retrieval, the amount of context, instructions and model choice, document governance (which version
is authoritative?), and abstention.
Document governance is a management problem, not a technical one: if two official policies contradict each other, no retrieval
system will resolve it.
Tools such as RAGChecker split errors into retriever and generator components (
).
Systematic evaluation is Session 5.

Relevant but incomplete evidence can still produce

=== PAGE 35 ===
Relevant is not enough: the passage may be incomplete, inconclusive, or contradicted by another.
In the HR example: the policy passage is relevant, but without the Ticino annex it is not sufficient for an employee in Lugano.
Definition: context is “sufficient” if it “contains all the necessary information to provide a definitive answer to the query and ‘insufficient’ if it
lacks the necessary information, is incomplete, inconclusive, or contains contradictory information” (
).
Google Research (2025): large models answer well when the context is sufficient, but often answer instead of abstaining when it is not.
Larger models “(Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT 4o, Claude 3.5) excel at answering queries when the context is sufficient, but often output
incorrect answers instead of abstaining when the context is not” (
).
Insufficient retrieved context can even make models more willing to answer: Gemma gave incorrect answers on 10.2% of questions
with no context, but 66.1% with insufficient context.
The blog puts it this way: adding context “paradoxically reduces the model’s ability to abstain from answering when appropriate”.
Their “selective generation” method combines a sufficiency check with the model’s confidence to decide when to answer.
It raised the share of correct answers among those given by 2–10%.
Google uses these ideas in a re-ranker of its Vertex AI RAG Engine.

=== PAGE 41 ===
Local questions have their answer in a few passages: classic retrieval works well.
Global questions (here, recurring supply-chain risks) have no passage containing the answer: it must be assembled from the whole
corpus.
Microsoft GraphRAG, simplified (
, preprint):
an LLM extracts entities and their relationships into a knowledge graph;
closely related entities are grouped into communities (themes);
each community is summarized;
the answer combines partial answers from the summaries.
Changing how the corpus is represented changes which questions it can answer, at a higher indexing cost.
On global questions over corpora of about a million tokens, it gave more comprehensive and diverse answers than vector RAG
(comprehensiveness win rates of 72–83% on podcast transcripts), while vector RAG gave the most direct answers.
Cost: building the graph requires many LLM calls. LazyGraphRAG defers that work to query time, with indexing costs “identical to
vector RAG and 0.1% of the costs of full GraphRAG” (
, vendor results).
Not automatically better: “recent studies report that GraphRAG frequently underperforms vanilla RAG on many real-world tasks” (
).
Use it for corpus-wide, relational questions; test it against plain RAG.

=== PAGE 45 ===
Retrieval itself becomes a reasoning task.
A multi-hop question needs several documents, where one reveals what to look for in the next (here, a contract number).
One retrieval step with the original question may not find the clause.
The example (fictional):
the supplier register shows that Rhône Transports is under framework contract FC-2024-019;
searching that contract finds clause 12.3: 0.5% of the invoice per day of delay.
“Agentic RAG […] enabl[es] LLM agents to actively decompose tasks, issue exploratory queries, and refine evidence through iterative
retrieval” (
).
An early version: Self-RAG trains a model to decide when to retrieve and to critique its own output (
).
In production: Google’s enterprise agentic RAG adds a “Sufficient Context Agent” so that the system “knows when it is missing
information and continues searching”, with accuracy up to 34% higher than standard RAG on factuality datasets (
, vendor results).

A known-answer question is one where you know, before asking, which passage or passages contain the answer, or that none does.
It lets you check retrieval separately from generation.
This is the test set you start building in today’s project work.
How to check today:
evidence: is the expected passage among those the model received?
groundedness: check every claim against the cited passage;
abstention: ask questions the documents do not cover;
answer quality: compare with the expected answer.
A fifth, business dimension: cost and latency.
What did the architecture buy us? Compare time and tokens per answer, before and after a change.
